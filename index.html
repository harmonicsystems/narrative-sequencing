        About this tool

        What it is

        A calm screen where a student puts the events of a short story in order, and a record for the clinician of what happened: the order placed, the time taken and the help given. At the end it writes an objective line ready to paste into a SOAP note.

        It runs in a web browser with nothing to install. There are no accounts and no student names, and nothing is saved: closing the page clears the session.

        Why it works the way it does

        • It records and does not judge. The order a student builds shows how they understand the story. A wrong order is information, not a failure, so the app never marks a card right or wrong. A first wrong try gets only "Try again".
        • Interpretation stays with the clinician. The app counts and reports. It does not score, label or diagnose.
        • Support is part of the data. The clinician gives help from a ladder of cues, lightest first: a verbal cue, placing the first or last card, narrowing the choice to two, a pointer that grows over the next card, or showing the order. Each one is logged on the attempt with its level (independent, verbal, visual, model), so a cued success is never written up as independent. The app never gives a cue on its own.
        • Sequencing leads to retell. Ordering the cards is the scaffold; telling the story is usually the goal. The retell step counts events told, prompts given, the order they came in, time words and cause words, which story parts (character, problem, feeling, action, ending…) the student included, and how much picture support was left on the table.
        • Low stimulation by design. Muted colours, no sounds, no rewards or animations, and pictures with no numbers, arrows or words that would give the order away.
        • Feelings without a right answer. In the feelings match, each picture was drawn as one feeling, but that is the illustrator's intent, not the answer. With open labels the student's choice or own word is recorded as given; with fixed words the log notes whether the choice matches the drawing. The student never sees right or wrong.
        • More than one way through. In Working it out, two friends reach a hard moment and the student chooses what could happen next. Every choice leads somewhere, some paths loop back, and one ending is that it is not mended yet. No choice is marked right or wrong: the app records the path taken, and any step can be gone back to. The idea came from a school counselor.
        • Stories that run both ways. Some stories (a kettle heating, a storm building, a voice getting louder) can be ordered forward or backward, to work on gradual change as well as routines.

        What the research suggests

        Narrative intervention research, much of it from the developers of the Story Champs program, points to a few practices that this tool tries to support without taking over the clinician's role:

        • Story grammar. Stories with a character, a problem, a feeling, an action and an ending. Five stories here are built that way, and each card names its part, so the retell line can say which parts were told.
        • Many examples of the same shape. Several short stories with the same parts, rather than one long one.
        • Pictures, then less. Retell first with pictures, then with words only, then with no cards. The retell records each step the clinician takes.
        • Time and cause words. Words like then and after, and because and so, are counted separately.

        Most of that research is with preschool to third grade, and much of it is by the program's own authors. Sources and caveats are in the research notes.

        How it was made

        I built this with Claude, Anthropic's AI model, working as a coding partner. I described what a session needs to look like and what the note needs to say; Claude wrote the code, and I tried each version and corrected it. The whole app is one web page with no outside services, which keeps student data on the device and makes the tool easy to host and to trust.

        The cartoon pictures were made with Google's Gemini image generator, one card at a time from written prompts, with each card built on the one before so the character and scene stay the same and only the event changes. The shape cards for the concept stories are simple drawings written directly as code.

        The five story grammar stories were drawn differently. Each child (Ana, Sam, Jo, Max), Gran and the puppy got a character sheet first; every card was then drawn by OpenAI's image model, through Codex, with those sheets attached and one master layout per story, so faces, rooms and sizes stay the same from card to card. The same characters then got feelings sheets, nine feelings drawn subtle, exaggerated, and with lines and colour, which became the pictures for the feelings match. I reviewed each round and asked for fixes: a face that looked angry instead of puzzled, a flashlight that changed colour, a backpack with a flap it shouldn't have.

        Who did what

        My part: the clinical design and every decision. What to measure and what to leave out. What the student should and should not see. How cues are named and logged. The wording of the objective line. Which stories to include and what each card must show. Trying each version as a clinician would, and saying what was unclear.

        Claude's part: the building. Writing and testing the code, drafting the image prompts and running the image generation, checking colour contrast and screen sizes, and keeping the documentation current. Claude also suggested features, such as the retell step, which I chose from.

        How much work went into it

        • Built across two days of working sessions, in about twenty rounds of "try it, change it".
        • 21 stories: 8 picture stories with 33 illustrated cards, 5 story grammar stories with 28 illustrated cards (one with two characters who feel differently about the same event), and 8 concept stories with 31 shape cards.
        • A feelings match for the whole cast: six characters, nine feelings each, drawn three ways (subtle, exaggerated, and with lines and colour), 162 pictures in all.
        • 2 branching stories for Working it out: 22 moments and 6 endings, in emoji and words so far.
        • Several rounds of rework on the pictures alone, to keep one child consistent across stories and to make neighbouring cards easy to tell apart.
        • Setup, card sizes, themes, the retell step, the cue ladder and this tour were each added in response to trying the tool and finding what got in the way.

        The speed came from the combination. Knowing what a session and a note require made it possible to give precise direction and to recognise a wrong answer quickly; Claude made it possible to act on each decision within minutes.
